MIT recently released the [report from their ad-hoc committee on AI
and education](https://aiandeducation.mit.edu/report/), which is a great read and worthy of the
"bold ideas" MIT is known for.  My colleague Matthew Rascoff, who just
ran a fascinating panel session at the [INSPIRE 2026
convening](https://calearninglab.org/inspire-2026/), interviewed the
committee's co-chairs, Sam Madden (head of CS there, and Berkeley
mafia :-) and Eric Klopfer (director of the Scheller Teacher Education
Program).

Matthew was kind enough to give me a pre-release listen of the
interview, which will be released as an episode in the Higher Ed
miniseries he's producing for the EdTech Insiders podcast. 
It goes live November 3, so I can't reproduce it here, but I took away
some interesting points from the interview, some of which are less
obvious in the report than others.

## The importance of humanism

The democratization of frighteningly powerful technology that can
change the world carries great responsibility.  Accepting that
responsibility requires understanding of history, culture, context.
Our students must be humanists.  Reading philosophy may not help you
directly "on the job," but it prepares you to read critically and
follow logical arguments.  Understanding history may not help you "on
the job," but it will give you context for the potential effects of
the things you are about to build.

This point also came up in the panel Matthew moderated, and our own
Narges Norouzi, who works at the intersection of AI and education and
is serving as Associate Dean in Berkeley's College of Computing,
Data Science, and Society, even went so far as to propose that a "4
year degree" be subdivided into (say) a 2-year degree in computing and
two 1-year degrees in the humanities or social sciences.  Berkeley's
[Data 8](data8.org) and [connector courses](https://ds-modules.github.io/curriculum-guide/connector/general/what-is-a-connector.html), and the associated [data
science modules](https://ds-modules.github.io/curriculum-guide/module/general/what-is-a-module.html) that [Eric Van Dusen](https://cdss.berkeley.edu/people/eric-van-dusen) and his team have been
creating, are a step in this direction.  And at the
[FISEE 2026](https://www.laser-foundation.org/fisee/2026_/) I recently
attended (and co-organized), this was one of the points that had broad
agreement even among those who disagreed vigorously about the
discipline-specific approaches for teaching software engineering in
the age of GenAI.

For a long time, a typical response to "students should be better
steeped in the humanities" was "it's so hard to learn the technical
stuff that there isn't enough time for that in a 4-year degree."  We
no longer have that excuse.

## Grades and transcripts as "legacy code"

Everyone seems to hate grades, and to agree that they create perverse
incentives and contribute to students' transactional view of education
("I'm here to get a job, for which I need a degree and good grades
from a good college").  So why do we still have them?
Matthew compared them to "legacy code": there's a whole ecosystem of
higher education and career paths that is built around the concept of
grades and transcripts, and we need to interoperate with it.
Sam Madden mentioned how at Brandeis students now get a second
["shadow
transcript"](https://www.forbes.com/sites/lisachambers/2026/08/13/this-college-now-verifies-students-real-world-skills-to-help-them-get-jobs/)
in which faculty or instructional staff attest to extracurricular or
co-curricular skills that don't emerge from the transcript alone, such
as internship skills, hackathons, etc.  Unlike a LinkedIn profile,
which a student could self-update, adding things to the shadow
transcript requires faculty sign-off.  Sam also told the story of a
particular Harvard political science professor who would give students
two grades at the end of his course: one that goes on the transcript
and ensures they'll have a good shot at law school, and a second
unofficial one that represents the professor's unvarnished and
annotated opinion of the student's work in the course.  (At least this
instructor is baldly admitting to grade inflation up front.  His
honesty should be more refreshing than it is.)

But again, this overlaps an INSPIRE topic--transcripts are lossy,
not useful as indicators of specific achievement, and create perverse
incentives, so we need to start putting mechanisms in place that will
lead to their long-term de-emphasis.

## Why "Socratic" AI tutors partially miss the point.

MIT, and its peer institutions (mine included), are working on all
manner of "Socratic" AI-based tutors that help students struggle with
material rather than enabling cognitive offloading.  But these only
work if students are actually motivated to learn rather than motivated
to get a particular grade in the easiest way possible.  My colleague
[Dan Garcia](www.cs.berkeley.edu/~ddgarcia) and I were discussing on
the way home from the workshop that the social contract with students
has broken down: the character of so many current students makes it
acceptable to commit acts of academic misconduct that would have been
unthinkable when we were undergraduates, and they have no remorse
about it when confronted---their main reaction is to avoid getting in
trouble.  It's gotten so bad that campus counsel has advised certain
faculty not to proceed with cases in which there was obvious and
blatant cheating, because forgetting to dot any I's or cross any T's
in the student misconduct procedure could land the university in an
expensive courtroom fight that it was unlikely to win anyway.  It is a
sad state of affairs when student cheating becomes the same kind of
moral dilemma as fighting organized crime.

Perhaps learning more about history and philosophy would improve this
aspect of students' character---but I'm not sure.

## AI tools aren't collaborative

Especially with AI able to do detail work like pounding code,
employers care more than ever about so-called "soft skills," including
collaboration skills, participating in or managing a team, and so on.
Yet AI tools as currently formulated are designed for individual use.
As Matthew pointed out, a lot of people work on collaborative software
projects where AI-assisted coding is happening; but the actual
collaboration is via existing mechanisms such as GitHub---not
facilitated by the AI tools themselves.

What could we do to specifically involve AI in the teaching and
practicing of collaboration skills?
