title: "Little Free Libraries, public libraries, or something else?"

---

Public libraries or Little Free Libraries®?

I love books.  I love libraries, both for their practical value and as
temples of learning and storehouses of knowledge over time.  I like
what libraries do, and how the nobility of their missions is often
reflected in their architecture, whether grand university libraries or
modest but sturdy "Carnegie libraries" in towns all across the US.
I'm proud of the fact that the publicly-supported, truly free (no cost
to subscribe or borrow) public library is basically an American
invention.  I actually get a tiny dose of optimism about humanity when
I enter a public library and see kids reading or being read to, people
using the free computers with Internet access, notices for free
services around résumé coaching and job placement in multiple
languages... you get the idea.

There's a bunch of LFLs in my neighborhood too.  The first time I saw
one of these I was charmed, and I've been a regular contributor to
(and borrower from) LFLs ever since.

Do these compete with each other?  If I want to disseminate good books
and encourage reading, is it a zero-sum game in supporting one over
the other?  Or does each have its place?

There isn't a lot of scholarly study of this, but there is some, and
one thing they agree on is that even though part of the LFLs' mission
is to irrigate "book deserts," in practice LFLs are [most abundant in
the neighborhoods that are already most affluent](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/eng_bookpubpaper/48/) and arguably least in
need of them.

There is one rather shrill
[scholarly paper](http://eprints.rclis.org/31499/1/VOR-2017-Schmidt-Hale.pdf) by
Schmidt and Hale arguing that "...the LFL® movement is an
example of the non-profit industrial complex (NPIC) in action, and, at street level, reminds
us that government funded public libraries are not to be taken for granted in an era of civic
crowdfunding where the privileged classes [defined as those meeting or above median household incomes in a given neighbourhood] feel emboldened to take control of traditionally
government-funded civic services."
My initial reaction to that is, fuck you.  I can assure you that when
I drop off a couple of books I'm finished with at one of the LFL
boxes, I am manifestly **not** intending to "take control of
traditionally government-funded civic services," as if I were somehow
trying to actively co-opt the services of the (excellent) San
Francisco Public Library, which also has a nice little branch in my
neighborhood.  I can't imagine the LFL founders are some kind of
nonprofit slumlords -- I genuinely believe their heart is in the same
place as mine: more books available to more people means more reading,
and more reading makes a more informed public which is better all
around.

The kind of polemic in this paper doesn't seem to help shed any
light on how LFLs and public libraries can or should work together to
avoid "competing" for individuals' support.
Perhaps more productive in an [article posted on the Wickson Library's
website](https://www.wicksonlibrary.org/post/10-little-facts-about-little-free-libraries)
is the statistic that there are 3 LFLs for every public library in the
US (although I'm sure they're not evenly distributed) that "while much
smaller than their public counterparts, tend to complement and
increase existing library offerings as they reach farther into the
community. No library cards, due dates, or fines!"  This last point is
a valid one:  some public library users get blocked for having
accumulated too many fines, so they turn to an LFL instead.  (Though
arguably LFLs shouldn't exist to enable this bad behavior,
where a better alternative would be to encourage some level of
accountability; even fine amnesty isn't necessarily needed to do
this.)
Another [article](https://americanlibrariesmagazine.org/2018/01/02/question-little-free-libraries/) reports that some public libraries
have sponsored LFL boxes and the programs are very popular with their
members, and in some cases have 
increased library-adjacent volunteerism and wider sense of ownership
of the library's mission; these seem like things that could ultimately benefit libraries.

There are also legitimate concerns about LFLs identified in these articles.
Hale and Schmidt found at least one case where LFLs **have** replaced libraries that either shut
down due to lack of funding or found themselves in a
position of having to charge for membership to pay the bills.  Having
LFLs fill that vacuum
sets the dangerous precedent that when the tax base is withdrawn,
privately-supported LFLs can and will pick up the slack.

As well, the selection and quality of LFL books isn't curated -- for
better or worse, it's stuff people are willing to get rid of.  (I can
imagine that the quality of books found depends heavily on the neighborhood.)

Most importantly, actual public libraries, as I suggested above,
provide far more than a repository of freely-loanable books: they
provide services of many kinds, activities, support for finding
information, literacy programs, and much more.  The irony of this is
illustrated in the below cartoon found on one of the LFL pages.

![Cartoon showing tiny librarian sitting inside LFL box]("https://static.wixstatic.com/media/ba5ed6_ff33a0a9e03f4614822016914eeedc58~mv2.jpg/v1/fill/w_740,h_387,al_c,lg_1,q_80,enc_avif,quality_auto/ba5ed6_ff33a0a9e03f4614822016914eeedc58~mv2.jpg")

SO...what do I do with the (generally high quality) books I'm willing
to part with?  My neighborhood is affluent, and surely most of the
people who live here could easily afford to buy these books
themselves.  (We have a great independent new & used bookstore right
around the corner from the branch library.)  I could donate them to
the Friends of the SF Public Library, but my understanding is most
donations are destined for fundraising book sales rather than going
into circulation.  Not that I claim to be a good judge of which books
_ought_ to go into circulation; I just want others to be able to
benefit from books I found useful, entertaining, or informative, but
that I no longer need.

What's your opinion?
